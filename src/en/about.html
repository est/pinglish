About this project.

It started with one Zhihu question and became a scheme, a converter and an archive. Here is where it came from, and what it is not.

If you read one paragraph: this is not a campaign to abolish pinyin, and it is not a linguistics paper. It is a public design exercise and a dossier about institutional cost.

01 / 06

What it is.

What are the obvious flaws in the Hanyu Pinyin scheme? That is a question on Zhihu. Answers have kept arriving since 2015. We turned the saved 39 answers into a searchable archive, and drew out three recurring themes:

Names: ü has three identities across passports, input methods and banking systems.
Phonology: i, e, o, ian and ong do not map one-to-one onto sounds.
Institutions: a system designed for 1958 was asked to do the work of 2026.

Then we did two things: turned the discussion into a readable narrative, and answered the question “if we started over, could it be better?”

02 / 06

Method.

Facts, opinions and design are kept apart.

STEP 01

Archive

Keep the text, author, upvotes, date, original link and images of all 39 answers. Do not edit the originals; only add summaries and filters at the display layer.

STEP 02

Separate

Distinguish factual claims, personal experience, normative arguments and concrete proposals. The opinions in the archive belong to their authors, not to this site.

STEP 03

Design

Turn the constraints into a testable alternative: rules, a syllable table, a converter and counterexample tests.

The alternative is called Pinglish. Its goal is not “more correct”, but “more coherent under a different set of constraints”: plain ASCII, no contractions, no rewrites, reversible, tone as digits. The full spec is on the scheme page; the implementation and tests are on GitHub.

04 / 06

Fact-check.

Some claims in the archive need a patch. These are the ones we checked before launch.

Common claimVerdictMore accurate wording
ü is “a German letter”, not an English letter. False ü is the Latin letter u plus a diaeresis. German, Turkish, Hungarian and Estonian use it as a letter; French, Spanish and older Portuguese use it to mark a separate vowel. Hanyu Pinyin borrows it for [y]; it is not “a German letter stuffed into an English alphabet”.
The passport spelling of Lü changed from LU to LYU in 2014/2015. Needs correction A 2012 public-security notice set Ü→YU (LYU/NYU); a 2021 National Immigration Administration announcement is the current basis and also sets LÜE/NÜE→LUE/NUE. Older passports usually show LU; LV is a keyboard/social spelling, not the official passport form.
Using v for ü or omitting tone marks is “illegal”. Depends v is a keyboard representation allowed by GF 3006-2001, not a formal spelling. Tone marks may be omitted in passports, proper names and indexes. Calling every informal use “illegal” conflates formal text with keyboard input.
The Shandong Education Department’s “2012 revision” is an official new scheme. False The document has no issuing body, document number or seal; it is a teacher’s document that circulated online and was reposted. The current national scheme remains the 1958 Hanyu Pinyin Scheme.
The 1958 scheme was meant to replace Hanzi. Needs qualification The scheme was approved by the First National People’s Congress on 11 February 1958. Zhou Enlai stated in January that it was not an alphabetic writing system intended to replace Hanzi. Some advocates did have a longer romanization project; in 1986 that project was formally stopped.
LYU is Cantonese romanization. False Cantonese 呂 is read leoi5 and is often romanized Lui. LYU comes from GB/T 28039 and the passport Ü→YU rule, not from Cantonese.
Bing Dwen Dwen / Shuey Rhon Rhon prove that pinyin has been abandoned. Overstated These are the Beijing Winter Olympics organisers’ official advertising transliterations, designed for English pronunciation and brand recognition, not standard pinyin. Chinese names, place names and the legal status of pinyin are unchanged.
Pinglish is a “better pinyin”. No It is a coherent design exercise under a specific set of constraints. It has not gone through language planning, education, publishing or standardisation. Its value is as a reference point, not a replacement.

05 / 06

What it is not.

It is not an official proposal, a language-planning recommendation, a rejection of current pinyin, or a show of disrespect to the designers of 1958. The more carefully you read the original debates, the more reasonable their choices look in context.

It is also not “an easier pinyin for foreigners”. No romanization will make an English speaker read Chinese names correctly on sight. Pinglish aims at internal consistency, reversibility and typeability, not cross-language spelling intuition.

One sentence

Pinyin does not need to be replaced.
It needs to be understood.

Understand why it looks the way it does, where it charges a cost, and what historical conditions produced each compromise. Then you can keep using it, or design something better.
