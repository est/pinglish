One name,
four spellings.

In the world of romanization, identity is not a philosophical question. It is a string of characters.

In 2016, a man surnamed Lü wrote on Zhihu that he had spent twenty years watching the same field on every form. He had renewed a passport, applied for credit cards, written letters to foreign schools, and explained again and again that he was still himself.

He is not alone. In this archive of 39 answers, a person named Xue is called Mr Zoo, Mr Shoe, Mr Shui. A Lü receives two credit cards, one spelling lv and one lu. Someone trying to type Xi'an gets xian instead.

“Who on earth thought it was a good idea to drag the German letter ü into a pinyin system made entirely of English letters?”

Anonymous · 735 upvotes · 2016

Treat that as a complaint and you miss the point. A set of letters designed in 1958 for typewriters, literacy classes and diplomacy is now doing identity verification for passports, databases, input methods and international conferences.

It is not broken. It is overloaded.

02 / 06

Four impossibilities,
1958.

The designers did think about it. They thought, and then they chose.

A

Accuracy

One letter for one sound. Ideally tone, the apical vowel and ü would all be visible.

B

Brevity

Pinyin must be quick to write and read. Digraph initials are already long enough; no more marks.

C

Twenty-six keys

No new letters. In 1958, a new letter meant a new typewriter, new type and new textbooks.

D

Foreign readability

j for English, q for French, x for Portuguese. Four languages cannot all be satisfied, but all were considered.

“A romanization has to serve four goals, and those goals contradict one another. They cannot all be reconciled.”

Asa Ha · 298 upvotes

In 1956, tens of thousands of people joined the debate. b/d/g or p/t/k? zh/ch/sh or ẑ/ĉ/ŝ? ü or y/iu/v? Were thirty letters enough? These are not later second-guesses; they are the minutes of the meetings.

Every time pinyin feels awkward, a compromise from 1958 is collecting interest.

03 / 06

One alphabet,
three jobs.

Annotation, typing, romanization. Three goals, three different optima.

JOB 01

Annotate Hanzi

It must be accurate, systematic and teachable. ü must exist, ian would be better as ien, and the i in zhi should differ from the i in ji.

accuracy first
JOB 02

Type on a keyboard

It must be short, unambiguous and touch-typeable. ü should be v, tone marks should disappear, and iu/ui/un should be contracted.

speed first
JOB 03

Write names for the world

It must be readable, survive a border check and fit in a database. ü should be yu, x/q should vanish, and tone marks should stay out.

readability first

The real problem

This is not a spelling problem.
It is a product-positioning problem.

A product designed in 1958 for annotation and literacy was asked to become an input method, an identity document, an international standard and a URL. Any product manager would say the requirements were wrong from the start.

If we started over.

We are not asking anyone to replace pinyin. We only want to show that another path exists.

PINGLISH / v1.0

One sound,
one spelling.

Pinglish is a thought experiment: what if 1958 had today’s keyboard? It keeps the sound system of Standard Chinese and rewrites every spelling rule: no ü, no tone marks, no y/w glides, no iu/ui/un contractions, no j/q/x.

One sentence / two spellings01
Wǒ xǐhuan chī Běijīng kǎoyā.
uo3 hi3 huan0 chy1 bei3 ging1 kau3 ia1.
I like eating Beijing roast duck.
Every syllable ends in a digit; every digit is a tone; every character is on the keyboard.
01

Plain ASCII

ü is written v; tone is written 1–4. Passports, URLs, databases and old terminals will not eat it.

02

No contractions

iou, uei and uen are always written out. No iu/ui/un, and no “the main vowel disappears when you mark the tone”.

03

No rewrites

au is au, ung is ung, ien is ien. Spelling and sound are not separated by history.

04

Reversible

407 Standard Chinese syllables, zero collisions. Any Pinglish string parses back to exactly one pinyin string.

A DISCLAIMER, BEFORE YOU SHARE THIS

This is not a campaign
to abolish pinyin.

We do not recommend that anyone replace the current system. It is written into dictionaries, passports, textbooks and several generations of muscle memory. This project wants to do one thing: show that another path exists, and make it legible.
